"In theory, there is no difference between theory and practice. In practice, there is." — Yogi Berra

To our partners, readers, and fellow students of the market,

There is a particular kind of excitement that every quantitative researcher experiences when their first backtest flashes across the screen. Numbers spiral upward. Returns compound. The equity curve looks like a rocket ship. For a brief, intoxicating moment, you believe you have cracked the code.

We experienced that moment too. Our initial V2 backtest generated returns exceeding +400% over five years.

Then we did what separates serious investors from reckless gamblers: we questioned it. We stress-tested it. We broke it apart piece by piece and held every assumption up to the light. And we discovered that our rocket ship was, in fact, a house of cards built on hidden biases and unrealistic assumptions.

This letter is the story of what we found, how we fixed it, and what our model looks like when the fantasy is stripped away and only honest mathematics remain. It is not a letter about triumphant returns. It is a letter about intellectual honesty, institutional rigor, and the relentless pursuit of a strategy that will actually survive contact with the real world.

We believe that is far more valuable.

Part I: Why We Abandoned the Moving Average

Our V1 strategy — a simple Moving Average Crossover system — operated on a single idea: if a stock's 20-day average price rises above its 50-day average price, buy it. If it falls below, sell it. It is one of the oldest trading rules in the book, taught in every introductory finance class, and used by retail traders worldwide.

It is also, in our assessment, fundamentally flawed as a primary signal.

Here is the core problem: Moving Average Crossovers are pure market timing tools. They attempt to predict whether the entire market is about to go up or down. This is an extraordinarily difficult task. The greatest investors in human history — Warren Buffett, Ray Dalio, George Soros — have spent entire careers disagreeing on when the market is about to turn. An algorithm built on a 20-day vs. 50-day crossover is making an implicit claim that it can time the market better than all of them.

We decided to stop trying to predict the market. Instead, we built a strategy that simply asks a better question.

Part II: The Philosophy Behind the V2 Composite Momentum Engine

Rather than asking "Is the market going up?", we started asking "Which stocks, right now, are the strongest performers relative to their peers?"

This is the foundation of Cross-Sectional Momentum — one of the most academically documented and institutionally respected strategies in all of quantitative finance. The idea was first formally proven by Professors Narasimhan Jegadeesh and Sheridan Titman in their landmark 1993 paper, and has since been independently verified across virtually every major equity market in the world. It is so robust that Eugene Fama and Kenneth French — the architects of modern asset pricing theory — were forced to add a dedicated Momentum Factor to their foundational model, because the anomaly simply couldn't be explained away.

The intuition behind momentum is deeply human. When a company is executing brilliantly — beating earnings, expanding margins, launching new products — institutional funds don't pour all their capital in overnight. They accumulate slowly, across weeks and months. This gradual, patient accumulation creates a sustained upward price trend. Momentum strategies exist to identify that trend and ride it before the broader market fully prices it in.

The V2 engine executes this through a Tri-Factor Composite Scoring System. On the final trading day of every month, the algorithm evaluates every stock in the index and scores it across three equally weighted dimensions:

Factor 1: Price Momentum (The Trend Signal)

We calculate the 6-month trailing return for every stock. Why 6 months? Research consistently demonstrates that the 3-to-12 month lookback period captures the "sweet spot" of momentum — long enough to filter out random daily noise, short enough to respond to genuine macroeconomic shifts. Stocks that have delivered the strongest raw returns over the trailing 6 months rank highest on this factor.

Think of this as identifying which athletes have been running the fastest race over the past half-year.

Factor 2: Inverse Volatility (The Quality Filter)

Price momentum alone can be dangerously misleading. A stock that surges 60% in two weeks and then crashes 50% looks like a momentum winner in the first moment but is a disaster in the second. Raw returns without a quality filter can chase exactly the wrong kind of explosive, unstable moves.

To correct for this, we invert the volatility signal. For each stock, we calculate the standard deviation of its daily returns over the trailing 6 months. Stocks with lower volatility receive a higher quality score. The algorithm systematically prefers smooth, sustained climbers over violent, unpredictable spikes.

In human terms: we do not want the stock that jumped 40% because of a viral tweet. We want the stock that has quietly, steadily, and persistently marched upward for six months.

Factor 3: Volume Surge (The Institutional Footprint)

Price and volatility tell us what a stock is doing. Volume tells us who is doing it.

Large institutional funds — pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, hedge funds managing billions — cannot enter or exit positions overnight without causing massive price disruption. Instead, they accumulate their positions gradually, over weeks or months. This quiet accumulation leaves a fingerprint: a slow, steady increase in trading volume that often precedes a sustained price breakout.

Our volume factor compares a stock's 1-month average volume to its 6-month average volume. A meaningful surge in volume alongside a rising price is a powerful signal that the "smart money" is moving in. Stocks exhibiting this pattern receive a higher composite score.

Portfolio Construction

The three factor scores are normalized and blended into a single Composite Score for every stock. The engine selects the Top 10 highest-scoring names, allocates exactly 10% of capital to each (Equal-Weight), and holds them without interference for the entire calendar month. On the first trading day of the next month, the process begins again — the algorithm sells what no longer qualifies and buys the new leaders.

No emotion. No discretion. No overrides.

Part III: The House of Cards — Confronting Our Biases

When our first backtest produced +400% returns, we did not celebrate. We recognized the number was almost certainly wrong. Institutional-grade quantitative research has a long, painful history of strategies that looked brilliant in backtests and were catastrophic in live markets. The graveyard is full of funds that confused data artifacts for genuine alpha.

We identified three critical biases embedded in our initial framework, each of which independently inflated our results. Correcting them required rebuilding significant portions of the engine from scratch.

Bias 1: Survivorship Bias — The Most Dangerous Illusion in Backtesting

This is arguably the most insidious and commonly overlooked error in quantitative research. Here is how it manifests:

When we initially downloaded "the Nifty 50" or "the S&P 500" for our backtest, we downloaded the current constituents — the 50 or 500 companies that are in the index today, in 2026. We then tested our strategy on those companies going all the way back to 2016.

The problem is that the companies in those indices today are the survivors. They are the ones that thrived, grew, and earned their spot. The companies that were kicked out — the ones that went bankrupt, were delisted, experienced catastrophic earnings collapses — are nowhere in our dataset. We inadvertently designed a strategy that traveled back in time with perfect foreknowledge of which companies would survive the next ten years.

Naturally, buying the survivors and avoiding the failures looks brilliant in hindsight. It is not investing genius. It is time travel. And it produces returns that are completely fabricated.

The Correction: We integrated a point-in-time historical constituent database. For every month in our backtest going back to 2016, the algorithm now retrieves the exact roster of companies that were actually in the index during that specific month. If a company was added to the Nifty 50 in March 2019, our model cannot invest in it before March 2019. If a company was delisted in 2020, our model correctly experiences that loss.

This one correction alone obliterated most of our "alpha." Which is exactly the point. We would rather have honest, modest results than dishonest, spectacular ones.

Bias 2: Frictionless Trading — The Academic Fantasy

Every finance textbook contains a quiet, dangerous assumption buried in its formulas: assume zero transaction costs.

This assumption exists because it makes the math cleaner. In the real world, it does not exist. Every time you buy or sell a stock, you pay for it. You pay broker commissions. You pay the bid-ask spread — the small gap between what sellers are asking and buyers are offering, which the market maker pockets. You pay in slippage — the unavoidable reality that when you place a large enough order, your own buying pressure nudges the price upward before your order fully fills.

These frictions are small on any individual trade. They compound ferociously across hundreds of trades over years.

The Correction: We hard-coded a 0.1% transaction friction into every buy and sell order in the backtest engine. Every monthly rebalance that liquidates old positions and purchases new ones is penalized at this rate. This number reflects a realistic estimate of combined broker fees and market slippage for a systematic equity strategy.

The impact of this correction was particularly brutal for our Indian strategy. We will detail exactly why in the next section.

Bias 3: The Tax Headwind — The Cost That Hides in Plain Sight

While our engine accounts for broker-level friction, there is a second layer of real-world cost that sits entirely outside the backtest: taxation.

In India, any stock held for less than 12 months and sold for a profit is subject to Short-Term Capital Gains (STCG) tax, currently set at 20% of the profit. Because our strategy sells and replaces positions every single month, every winning trade in a live Indian fund would trigger STCG.

Consider what this means mathematically. If our Nifty strategy generates +8% in a month on a given position, the government takes 20% of that gain — reducing the effective return to roughly +6.4%. Compounded across 12 months, year after year, this tax drag compounds into a significant headwind that our backtest does not capture.

We deliberately chose not to hard-code STCG into the backtesting engine. This is standard institutional practice: because tax situations vary enormously by investor type (a domestic retail investor pays 20%; a foreign institutional investor may have different treaty obligations; a tax-exempt pension fund pays nothing), fund managers universally report performance Net-of-Fees but Pre-Tax, and separately disclose the tax implications.

We report our performance on the same basis. However, we acknowledge it explicitly, because any serious deployment of this strategy in a live Indian account must account for this drag.

Part IV: X-Raying the Model — Four Institutional Diagnostics

With biases corrected and transaction costs integrated, we subjected the surviving model to four institutional-grade analytical tools. The purpose was not to generate more impressive numbers. The purpose was to understand exactly what the strategy is, what it is not, and where its risks lie.

Diagnostic 1: Turnover Analysis — The Silent Wealth Destroyer

Turnover is the percentage of a portfolio's holdings that are replaced during a given period. High turnover means high trading frequency, which means high transaction costs. For a strategy already facing the 0.1% friction penalty, turnover is the primary lever that determines whether a strategy lives or dies after costs.

We calculated the monthly turnover rate for both portfolios.

The Indian (Nifty 50) Strategy: Monthly turnover of 42%. This means that on average, the algorithm was replacing four or five of its ten positions every single month. At 10% weight per position, replacing 4 positions means selling 40% of the portfolio and rebuying 40% — paying the 0.1% friction penalty on 80% of the portfolio's value every single month. Over a year, this adds up to approximately 2.4% in pure transaction drag on an annualized basis. Against the Indian bull market's strong baseline returns, this drag was devastating. The Nifty strategy's Sharpe Ratio — the primary measure of risk-adjusted return — came in at a modest 0.43.

The US (S&P 500) Strategy: Significantly lower turnover, and correspondingly, transaction costs that the strategy could absorb comfortably. The result: a Sharpe Ratio of 0.76 — a genuinely strong result for an equity momentum strategy.

The lesson here is profound, and it applies far beyond quantitative finance: efficiency is not just about what you earn, but about what you lose in the process of earning it.

Diagnostic 2: Fama-French Factor Regression — Is Our Alpha Real?

Here is a question that every fund manager must be able to answer: Is our performance the product of genuine skill, or are we simply riding factors that any investor could access cheaply?

If our strategy just happened to be fully invested in the stock market during a bull run, it would show high returns — not because of our algorithm, but because the market went up. If we just happened to hold small-cap value stocks, we would capture the well-known Value and Size premiums — again, not our own skill.

To isolate genuine "alpha" from known market factors, we ran a Fama-French Multi-Factor Regression. This academic framework — developed by Nobel Prize-winning economist Eugene Fama and his collaborator Kenneth French — decomposes a portfolio's returns into contributions from known, accessible risk factors: market exposure, size, value, and momentum. Whatever return remains after accounting for all these factors is true, unexplained alpha.

Our US strategy results:

  • Market Beta: 0.66 — Our portfolio moved 34% less than the overall market. In a market that dropped 10%, we would typically fall only 6.6%. This is a remarkably defensive characteristic for an equity strategy.
  • Alpha: +0.31% per month — After fully accounting for all known Fama-French factors, our strategy still generated 0.31% per month of genuine, unexplained excess return. Annualized, this represents approximately 3.7% of true alpha per year.

This is not a trivial number. In a world where most active fund managers fail to generate any statistically significant alpha at all, a well-constructed systematic strategy generating consistent, risk-adjusted alpha is precisely what institutional investors pay billions to find.

Diagnostic 3: Regime Analysis — How Does the Strategy Behave When the World Falls Apart?

Perhaps the most revealing diagnostic of all is Regime Analysis: separating the backtest data into distinct market environments (Bull markets and Bear markets) and measuring performance in each separately.

This test answers the most important question in investing: When the market crashes, does your strategy protect capital, or does it crash with everything else?

The results were striking.

During Bull Markets: The strategy performed broadly in line with the market, capturing most of the upside from rising momentum stocks. This is expected — when everything is going up, momentum stocks go up too.

During Bear Markets: Our strategy generated +1.79% Alpha per month relative to the benchmark. When the broader market was falling, our momentum selection process systematically rotated toward defensive, high-quality names that preserved capital.

This bear-market outperformance is the most institutionally significant result in our entire analysis. A strategy that protects capital during drawdowns is exponentially more valuable than one that merely earns higher returns during calm conditions — because the mathematics of recovery are unforgiving. Losing 50% of your capital requires a 100% gain just to break even. A strategy that loses only 25% in a crash requires only a 33% gain to recover. The strategy that protects capital in bad times, therefore, wins over full market cycles even if it slightly underperforms in good times.

Diagnostic 4: Sector Concentration — Knowing Your Largest Risk

Full transparency requires identifying not just what is working, but where your biggest vulnerabilities are.

Our Composite Momentum algorithm does not include any explicit sector constraints. It simply follows the scores wherever they lead. And where they consistently lead, in both the Indian and US universes, is heavily toward Technology.

During our backtest period, the algorithm's Top 10 selections frequently concentrated as much as 60-70% of the portfolio in Technology stocks — software companies, semiconductor manufacturers, e-commerce platforms. This is not a flaw in the algorithm's logic. It is a direct reflection of the past decade's macroeconomic reality: technology companies have been the dominant momentum leaders in global equity markets since roughly 2016.

However, it is a risk that must be explicitly acknowledged. A sustained sector rotation away from Technology — driven by rising interest rates, regulatory action, or any number of macro forces — would disproportionately hurt this strategy relative to a diversified benchmark. Our portfolio is not a diversified portfolio. It is a concentrated bet on the continuation of the current momentum regime.

We consider it our obligation to state this clearly.

Part V: Going Live — The First Month in the Wild

A backtest, however rigorously constructed, is ultimately a simulation. It is a controlled experiment in a laboratory. Markets, however, are not laboratories. They are chaotic, reflexive, and frequently irrational in the short term.

The only true test of a quantitative strategy is out-of-sample, forward-testing performance — running the algorithm on real market data that it has never seen before.

On July 31st, 2026, we deployed the V2 engine into a fully automated live paper-trading environment. We built an automated infrastructure on GitHub Actions — a cloud-based server that wakes up autonomously at 3:00 AM IST every trading night, fetches the official Closing Auction Session (CAS) prices from both the National Stock Exchange and NYSE, updates the live portfolio, and records the results to a permanent log. Every morning, a new data point is added to our out-of-sample dataset without any human intervention.

Contextualizing the Results: Two Very Different Stories

The data tells a nuanced, split story across our two portfolios — and each half deserves careful interpretation.

The Indian Strategy: A Quiet Win Our Nifty 50 Composite Momentum strategy returned -0.87% in August, against a Nifty 50 benchmark return of -1.34%. This means we outperformed the Indian market by +0.47 percentage points in a month where the entire index declined. In institutional terms, this is called generating positive alpha during a drawdown — precisely the behavior our Regime Analysis predicted. When the Nifty fell, our model's quality filters (Inverse Volatility and Volume Surge) had concentrated capital into the more resilient, institutionally-backed names in the index, cushioning the blow better than a passive index fund would have.

The US Strategy: A Sector Rotation Headwind Our S&P 500 Composite Momentum strategy returned -1.62%, against a benchmark return of +1.89% — a tracking error of -3.51 percentage points. This is a meaningful gap, and it deserves a clear-eyed explanation rather than a dismissal.

August 2026 was a textbook case of what quantitative researchers call a Momentum Factor Reversal. Here is precisely what happened:

For the better part of 2025 and the first half of 2026, the dominant market narrative was Technology. Large-cap US tech — Amazon, NVIDIA, Microsoft, Apple, AMD — generated extraordinary momentum scores. They had the strongest trailing 6-month returns, the smoothest price appreciation curves, and the most significant volume accumulation. Our algorithm correctly identified them and loaded the portfolio accordingly.

In August, institutional investors triggered what is colloquially called a "risk-off rotation." Having accumulated enormous gains in tech throughout the year, large funds began de-risking — selling tech to take profits and rotating into defensive, lower-volatility sectors: Consumer Staples (Coca-Cola, Procter & Gamble), Utilities, Healthcare, and Real Estate Investment Trusts. These sectors — the so-called "value laggards" — had underperformed all year and suddenly became the market's preferred destination.

Because the S&P 500 is a market-cap-weighted index holding all 500 companies, it captured the broad gains from this rotation. Every sector contributed positively to the benchmark's +2.60% return.

Our strategy, by design, was not in those sectors. Our model had correctly identified that, as of July 31st, the Technology names still held the highest composite momentum scores. The rotational selloff in tech happened after our monthly snapshot. We were holding last month's winners during this month's sector reversal.

This is not a model failure. It is a known, well-documented characteristic of momentum strategies — called "momentum reversal risk" — that every academic paper on the subject acknowledges. Cross-sectional momentum is exceptionally powerful over 3-to-12 month horizons, but it is vulnerable to sudden, sharp reversals in the short term.

What matters is how the strategy responds. And on September 1st, when our algorithm ran its monthly rebalance, it demonstrated exactly the kind of disciplined, systematic behavior it was designed for.

The September 1st Rebalance: Algorithmic Discipline in Action

After running the composite scores across the full S&P 500 universe on September 1st, the algorithm made its decision: do nothing. Zero changes. Zero turnover.

The same 10 US stocks that defined the August portfolio — AAPL, NVDA, AMZN, AMD, LLY, MSFT, KO, MRK, V, BAC — retained the highest composite scores after the September evaluation. Despite the temporary August pullback, their 6-month momentum signals, volatility characteristics, and volume footprints remained superior to every other name in the index.

By holding the winners, the algorithm avoided paying transaction friction on a complete portfolio liquidation and rebuy. It recognized that a one-month rotational pause does not invalidate a six-month upward trend. It waited.

For the Indian Nifty 50 strategy, the rebalance was equally efficient: one single substitution. Bajaj Finance exited. Its composite score had degraded — declining momentum over the trailing period, rising volatility, and normalized volume. Adani Enterprises entered, demonstrating accelerating price momentum and strong institutional volume accumulation. Nine stocks remained unchanged. Turnover was 10%. Transaction costs were minimal.

This is the algorithm working precisely as intended. Not overtrading. Not chasing noise. Cutting what has genuinely weakened, keeping what remains strong, and doing so with surgical efficiency.

Closing Thoughts: The Honest Ledger

We began this quarter chasing a ghost. Four hundred percent backtested returns sounded like success. They were a warning sign.

What we built instead is something more valuable: a rigorously honest framework. One that accounts for the stocks that no longer exist. One that pays its transaction costs. One that acknowledges its tax exposure. One that has been subjected to academic-grade regression analysis, regime testing, and honest discussion of its concentrated sector risk.

The result is a model that generated genuine, explainable alpha of +0.31% per month on a beta-adjusted basis over five years in the US market. A model that historically protected capital with +1.79% excess alpha per month during market downturns. A model that, in its first live month, underperformed in a way that is completely consistent with known, documented momentum factor behavior — and responded with exactly the disciplined, low-cost rebalancing that good systematic investing demands.

We did not beat the market in August. We do not pretend otherwise.

But we built something honest. And in investing, as in most of life, honesty compounded over time is worth more than any single month's performance.

The algorithm runs tonight, as it has every night since July 31st. It will run again tomorrow. And we will be here, reporting everything it finds — the good, the bad, and the uncomfortable.
